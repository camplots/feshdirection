THE INDUSTRY

WHAT THE NUMBERS SAY.

Record battery installations — and a regulator finding problems with most of the systems it inspects.

SEE THE NUMBERS ↓

Australia is installing home batteries faster than it has ever installed anything like them. At the same time, the regulator that oversees the rebate is finding problems with a majority of the installations it inspects.

Both of those are true. This page is where we keep the numbers straight — and where we'll update them as they move.

THE SCALE

478,176

home batteries installed in the twelve months from 1 July 2025 — 13.58 GWh of storage, across households and small businesses.

The era of the battery as a premium add-on is over. It's now the main event.

THE COMPLIANCE PICTURE

The Clean Energy Regulator inspects a statistically significant sample of battery installations that claim the federal rebate.

As at 30 June 2026, it had inspected 3,425 — 718 of them in Queensland.

Read that carefully — because the regulator is careful about it:

THE REGULATOR'S WORDS

"Substandard" does not mean the whole system is a dud. Typically, it is one or two items found in the installation that does not affect performance but may affect those working on or around the system in future.

The CER also cautions that it's early days: "It's too early to draw strong conclusions about the rate of technical compliance under the scheme."

Queensland's rate is the highest of the states the report covers.

But here is the finding that should matter most to anyone buying right now:

THE KEY FINDING

No installation had a problem with the battery itself. Every issue was non-compliant installation practice and substandard workmanship. — Clean Energy Regulator

And the most common failure isn't exotic. It's labelling:

  • The warning label at the main switchboard and any intermediate distribution board
  • Correct labelling of backed-up circuits — the ones still live when the main switch is off
  • The green reflective "ES" label that tells emergency services a battery is on site

Then: incorrectly configured or missing RCDs, insufficient mechanical and fire protection, and inadequate overcurrent protection for inverter circuits.

None of this is visible in a photo of a tidy-looking install. All of it matters to the electrician who works on your switchboard in ten years — and to the firefighter who arrives at your house at 2am.

THE INCENTIVE CLOCK

The federal Cheaper Home Batteries Program is the main subsidy for storage in Australia. It's delivered through STCs, and its value steps down twice a year on a published schedule:

PeriodSTC factor
January – April 20268.4
May – December 20266.8
January – June 20275.7
July – December 20275.2
… to December 20302.1

Since 1 May 2026, the discount is also tiered by capacity. Support is strongest on the first 14 kWh of usable storage, and falls away sharply above that. The rebate applies to the first 50 kWh of usable capacity, and eligible systems run from 5 to 100 kWh.

The practical consequence: the same battery costs more every six months. Waiting isn't neutral — it's a decision with a price on it.

In Queensland specifically:

  • The former state Battery Booster program closed in May 2024. There is no standalone Queensland battery rebate today.
  • The Queensland Government's Supercharged Solar for Renters scheme (opened December 2025) offers landlords up to $3,500 to install solar on a rental property.
  • Home Energy Support offers up to $2,500 to eligible concession-card holders.
  • The legacy 44-cent Solar Bonus Scheme feed-in tariff ends on 1 July 2028.

WHY BATTERIES, NOT PANELS, ARE NOW THE STORY

For most of the last decade, solar paid for itself through a combination of self-consumption and feed-in tariffs. Feed-in tariffs have collapsed. Retailers now pay roughly 3–8 cents per kWh for exported solar, while peak grid electricity costs 30–45 cents.

THE BATTERY ARGUMENT

That gap — roughly six to one — is the entire battery argument. A battery earns its keep by displacing the most expensive power you'd otherwise buy.

Which is why the household matters more than the hardware. A battery sized to a low-usage home earns back less, amortises over fewer cycles, and takes longer to pay off. Averages hide that. Your bill doesn't.

HOW PEOPLE GET SOLD TO

Solar is a high-value, once-a-decade purchase made by people who've never bought one before — which makes it a magnet for poor practice.

Two models dominate residential lead generation:

REFERRAL MARKETPLACES

  • You submit one enquiry
  • The service sells it to installers, sometimes several at once
  • The service is paid per lead regardless of whether the work is any good
  • The installer's cost of acquiring you gets built into the price you're quoted

DIRECT SALES

  • In-home appointments
  • Rebate-deadline urgency
  • A quote prepared on the spot

Consumer advocates have documented the recurring problems: a lazy, one-size-fits-all quote; an offer well below the going rate that usually means cheaper components; a "free eligibility check" that's really a lead-capture form; and baiting with quality equipment before swapping it for something cheaper at signing.

None of that is universal — there are excellent installers using both models. But the failure modes are consistent enough to be worth knowing before anyone knocks on your door.

WHAT'S CHANGING

The industry is being tightened up, which is good news for homeowners who know what to look for:

01

The regulator is acting

In one quarter (April–June 2026) the CER suspended 21 companies from the scheme, and it is expanding its inspection program. New photo evidence requirements took effect on 1 March 2026 specifically to lift labelling compliance.

02

Accreditation moved

Solar Accreditation Australia took over installer accreditation from the Clean Energy Council on 29 May 2024. It has separate classes for grid-connected PV, grid-connected battery systems, and stand-alone systems — and a solar accreditation does not qualify someone to install a battery.

03

The battery standard got harder

Amendment 1 to AS/NZS 5139 became mandatory on 19 December 2025, changing location, clearance and ventilation requirements for battery systems.

04

Inverter connections tightened

AS/NZS 4777.1:2024 has applied to new low-voltage inverter connections since 23 February 2025.

05

Consumer protection exists

The New Energy Tech Consumer Code now covers 2,000+ approved sellers — though it's voluntary, and not every seller has signed up.

06

Products can still be recalled

In November 2025, one approved battery controller was recalled on electrical safety grounds — a reminder that "on the approved list" is a starting point, not a guarantee.
