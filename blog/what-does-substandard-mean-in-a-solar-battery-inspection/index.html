What does “substandard” mean in a solar-battery inspection?

The Clean Energy Regulator reports that 62.28% of inspected battery installations were rated substandard. That figure needs context.

Watts Better · September 2026

The Clean Energy Regulator's solar-battery inspection results reported that, as at 30 June 2026:

  • 62.28% of inspected installations were rated substandard
  • 0.76% were rated unsafe
  • 36.95% were rated adequate

The figures relate to installations selected for the regulator's inspection program. They are not a survey of every battery installation in Australia.

The regulator also says it is too early to draw strong conclusions about the overall rate of technical compliance.

What does substandard mean?

In the regulator's terminology, substandard means technical non-compliance that requires rectification but is safe to remain in operation.

It does not necessarily mean:

  • The battery itself is defective
  • The system cannot produce electricity
  • The entire installation is unsafe
  • The homeowner must immediately switch the system off

The regulator explains that a substandard result may involve one or two installation items that do not affect performance but may affect people working on or around the system in the future.

What problems were found?

The most common issue was labelling.

Examples include:

  • Missing or incorrect warning labels
  • Incorrect labelling of backed-up circuits
  • Missing or incorrectly positioned energy-storage labels for emergency services

Other issues included:

  • Incorrectly configured or missing residual current devices
  • Insufficient mechanical or fire protection
  • Inadequate overcurrent protection
  • Loose connections showing signs of heat
  • Electrical work that did not meet applicable requirements
  • Neutral continuity problems on alternative-supply circuits

The regulator reported that no installations had issues with the battery itself. The issues were associated with installation practices and workmanship.

Why does an independent inspection matter?

A system can look complete from the ground while important details remain difficult for a homeowner to assess.

An independent inspection can provide:

  • A record of the areas assessed
  • Photographic evidence
  • An assessment of accessible installation work
  • Identification of matters requiring attention
  • A formal report for your records

It does not replace the installer's responsibilities, required certification or government inspection programs.

It gives you evidence about your completed installation rather than requiring you to assume everything is correct.

What should homeowners ask before installation?

Ask:

  • Who is responsible for the installation?
  • Which accredited person will sign off the work?
  • Does the installer hold the relevant battery accreditation?
  • What documentation will be provided?
  • What happens if an inspection identifies an issue?
  • Will the installer cooperate with independent inspection?

A completed system should be more than switched on. You should know what was assessed and where the evidence is.
