Why some solar panels cost so much less

Lower panel prices have made solar more accessible. But what are you giving up when one panel costs significantly less than another?

Watts Better · September 2026

Solar panels have become dramatically cheaper over the last decade.

That has been good for homeowners.

Lower panel prices have made rooftop solar more accessible and helped accelerate deployment around the world.

But low prices also create an important question:

What are you giving up when one panel costs significantly less than another?

The answer is not always quality.

Sometimes the price difference reflects manufacturing scale, technology, supply-chain efficiency or purchasing volume.

But extreme price competition can also create pressure on:

  • Materials
  • Quality control
  • Research and development
  • Factory utilisation
  • Warranty reserves
  • Local service
  • Manufacturer financial stability

A panel is expected to generate electricity for decades.

The company behind the warranty may not remain in the same position for that long.

China dominates solar manufacturing

China has become the dominant global manufacturing base for solar panels and their components.

The International Energy Agency reports that China's share of every major solar manufacturing stage — including polysilicon, ingots, wafers, cells and modules — exceeds 80%.

The IEA also reports that China invested more than US$50 billion in new PV manufacturing capacity over the last decade.

This dominance was built through:

  • Large-scale manufacturing
  • Vertical supply chains
  • High domestic demand
  • Heavy investment
  • Rapid technology upgrades
  • Integrated production of polysilicon, wafers, cells and modules
  • Strong export capability
  • Source: International Energy Agency — Solar PV Global Supply Chains

China's dominance does not automatically mean that Chinese panels are poor quality.

Many major Chinese manufacturers produce modules that are widely deployed and perform strongly in independent testing.

The more important issue is the structure of the market supporting those products.

The market has a serious overcapacity problem

Manufacturers built enormous production capacity during a period of strong solar growth.

Demand continued to expand, but manufacturing capacity expanded even faster.

That created a surplus of:

  • Polysilicon
  • Wafers
  • Cells
  • Modules
  • Factory capacity

When supply is much greater than demand, manufacturers have a choice:

  • Reduce production
  • Close or suspend factories
  • Consolidate
  • Sell inventory at lower prices
  • Continue producing at very thin margins
  • Take orders at a loss to preserve market share

Wood Mackenzie reported that leading Chinese solar manufacturers recorded a combined loss of approximately US$5.5 billion in 2025, despite strong shipment volumes.

That does not mean every manufacturer is losing money.

It does mean the sector is under severe financial pressure.

  • Source: Wood Mackenzie — Global solar PV module manufacturer ranking

Why a price war can affect quality

A price war does not automatically produce bad products.

Large manufacturers can reduce cost through:

  • Scale
  • Automation
  • Vertical integration
  • Purchasing power
  • Process improvements
  • Efficient logistics
  • Higher factory utilisation

But sustained pressure can create incentives to reduce cost wherever possible.

That may affect:

  • Glass thickness
  • Encapsulant selection
  • Backsheet or glass-glass construction
  • Junction boxes
  • Connectors
  • Soldering
  • Cell interconnection
  • Quality-control sampling
  • Factory maintenance
  • Warranty reserves
  • Research and development
  • Technical and after-sales support

The problem is that a reduction in quality may not be visible when the panel is installed.

It may appear later as:

  • Higher degradation
  • Delamination
  • Microcracks
  • Hotspots
  • Moisture ingress
  • Junction-box failure
  • Connector problems
  • Output loss
  • Difficult warranty claims

A panel can look perfect on installation day and still create a long-term risk.

Quality is not the same as country of origin

It would be wrong to treat all Chinese manufacturers as low quality.

Independent testing regularly identifies products from Chinese manufacturers among high-performing modules.

The 2026 PV Module Index from RETC recognised a range of manufacturers, including Chinese, Indian and other international brands, for high achievement in different reliability and performance tests.

The same report also highlighted ongoing industry-wide concerns involving:

  • Ultraviolet-induced degradation
  • Damp-heat performance
  • Thermal cycling
  • Hail durability
  • Differences between module designs and bills of materials
  • Source: RETC — 2026 PV Module Index

The correct question is not:

“Is this panel Chinese?”

The better questions are:

  • Which manufacturer made it?
  • Which factory produced it?
  • Which exact model is quoted?
  • Which bill of materials applies?
  • Has that model been independently tested?
  • How long has the model been in the market?
  • Who supports the warranty in Australia?
  • Is the manufacturer financially stable enough to support a long warranty?

The patent story is more complicated

There have been significant patent disputes in the solar industry.

For example, Hanwha Q CELLS filed patent-infringement complaints in the United States against JinkoSolar, LONGi Solar and REC Group involving passivation technology.

There have also been later patent settlements and cross-licensing arrangements between major manufacturers.

In 2026, LONGi and JinkoSolar announced a global settlement involving cross-licensing of selected core patents.

There have also been separate licensing arrangements involving companies such as Maxeon and Aiko.

  • Sources: Hanwha Q CELLS patent complaint; LONGi and JinkoSolar global settlement

But the evidence does not support saying that all Chinese manufacturers have infringed patents or that every panel carries a universal fee per panel sold in the United States or Europe.

Patent rights are:

  • Technology-specific
  • Company-specific
  • Jurisdiction-specific
  • Subject to litigation
  • Sometimes settled confidentially
  • Sometimes resolved through cross-licensing
  • Sometimes challenged or invalidated

For a homeowner, the practical issue is not to investigate every patent dispute.

It is to ask whether the manufacturer and importer have a credible right to sell the product in the relevant market and whether the contract provides appropriate support if a legal or supply-chain problem arises.

The warranty may outlive the manufacturer

Solar panels are often sold with:

  • Product warranties
  • Performance warranties
  • Degradation guarantees
  • Long-term output promises

These warranties may extend for 20, 25 or 30 years.

But the company providing the warranty may:

  • Be acquired
  • Leave the Australian market
  • Close a factory
  • Change distributors
  • Restructure
  • Become insolvent
  • Stop supporting an older model
  • Transfer responsibility to another entity

A warranty is only practically valuable if there is a functioning party capable of responding to a claim.

This is why manufacturer financial strength matters.

Solar-sector financial analysts such as Sinovoltaics assess manufacturers because financial distress can affect the credibility of long-term product warranties.

  • Source: Sinovoltaics — 2026 financial stability ranking

A 25-year warranty from a company that may not exist in five years is not equivalent to a 25-year warranty from a financially stable manufacturer with an Australian support network.

“Tier 1” does not mean perfect

Many homeowners see a “Tier 1” reference and assume it means:

  • Highest quality
  • Lowest failure rate
  • Best warranty
  • Guaranteed long-term support

That is not what bankability rankings are designed to prove.

A bankability rating may provide useful information about market presence, financing and manufacturer acceptance.

It does not replace checking:

  • Independent reliability tests
  • Exact model
  • Exact bill of materials
  • Warranty exclusions
  • Degradation terms
  • Australian service arrangements
  • Product availability
  • Installer quality

Do not treat a manufacturer ranking as a substitute for technical and commercial due diligence.

Australian product approval is not a long-term quality guarantee

For an Australian rebate-eligible installation, the relevant modules and inverters must meet applicable product and program requirements.

Product approval is important.

It helps establish that the product meets the relevant safety and compliance requirements for the program.

But approval does not guarantee:

  • That the manufacturer will remain in business
  • That every installation will be good
  • That the panel will never fail
  • That the warranty process will be easy
  • That the product is the best choice for your roof
  • That the installer will provide long-term support

Approval is a starting point, not a complete quality assessment.

What to check before accepting a panel

Ask for the exact:

  • Manufacturer
  • Model number
  • Factory or production series, where available
  • Rated power
  • Efficiency
  • Product warranty
  • Performance warranty
  • Degradation schedule
  • Warranty transfer rules
  • Warranty claim process
  • Australian importer or distributor
  • Australian contact details
  • Independent reliability test results
  • Product approval status

Also ask whether the quoted panel may be substituted if it becomes unavailable.

If the exact model changes, the installer should explain the replacement and obtain your approval before installation.

What does a long performance warranty really promise?

A performance warranty may promise that a panel will retain a certain percentage of its original output after a specified period.

That is not the same as guaranteeing:

  • Your total system output
  • Your annual savings
  • Your electricity bill
  • Your inverter performance
  • Your installation quality
  • Your roof condition
  • Your feed-in income

Performance claims can also require:

  • Technical testing
  • Proof of underperformance
  • Specific claim procedures
  • Access to the installation
  • Confirmation that the failure is attributable to the module
  • Compliance with warranty conditions

Ask how a performance claim would actually be proven.

The lowest price is not always the best value

A lower-cost panel may be a perfectly reasonable choice if:

  • The manufacturer is credible
  • The model has a good testing record
  • The warranty is clear
  • Australian support is available
  • The installer stands behind the system
  • The price reflects genuine manufacturing efficiency

But a low price deserves questions if it is achieved through:

  • An unclear manufacturer
  • A short market history
  • Weak local support
  • Unclear warranty responsibility
  • An unknown substitution policy
  • A distributor with no long-term presence
  • Claims that cannot be independently verified

The issue is not whether the panel is made in China.

The issue is whether the product, manufacturer, importer and installer can support the asset over time.

Questions to ask your installer

Ask:

  • Why has this panel been selected?
  • What is the exact model?
  • Who is the Australian importer?
  • Who will handle a warranty claim?
  • What happens if the manufacturer stops trading?
  • What independent testing has been completed on this model?
  • Is the quoted model on the applicable approved product list?
  • Can the installer substitute a different model?
  • Does the substitute require your approval?
  • What is the product warranty?
  • What is the performance warranty?
  • How are degradation claims tested?
  • What evidence is required?
  • Who pays for removal and reinstallation during a claim?
  • What happens if the panel is no longer available?

A good installer should be able to answer these questions without treating them as an inconvenience.

The bottom line

China's dominance has helped make solar cheaper and faster to deploy.

That is a major achievement.

But extreme overcapacity and price competition can create financial pressure across the manufacturing sector.

The result is not that every low-cost Chinese panel is poor quality.

The result is that homeowners should not confuse:

  • Low price with good value
  • High volume with financial strength
  • A long warranty with practical protection
  • Product approval with long-term support
  • A manufacturer ranking with a quality guarantee

The right question is:

Who will still be able and willing to support this panel when something goes wrong years after installation?
