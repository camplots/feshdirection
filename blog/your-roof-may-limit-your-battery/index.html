Your roof may limit your battery — here's where bidirectional charging fits

A battery cannot create more solar energy than your roof allows. If your household needs more stored energy than your roof can produce, bidirectional EV charging may be part of the answer.

Watts Better · September 2026

When your EV could become the next battery

A home battery can store solar energy.

But it cannot create more solar energy than your roof, panels, orientation, shading and system design allow.

This creates an important relationship:

The size of the solar system influences how much battery capacity your home can regularly use.

If your household needs a larger amount of stored energy than your roof can produce, simply adding more stationary battery capacity may not produce the result you expect.

That is where bidirectional charging may become important.

A battery does not create energy

A battery stores energy generated elsewhere.

For a solar home, that energy usually comes from:

  • Rooftop solar
  • The electricity grid
  • A combination of solar and grid charging

If the roof is already full, you may not be able to add enough solar panels to support a much larger battery.

The limitation may come from:

  • Available roof area
  • Roof orientation
  • Shading
  • Roof structures
  • Panel layout
  • Inverter capacity
  • Network export limits
  • Switchboard capacity
  • Local planning or installation constraints

The Australian Government notes that available sunny roof area can be a limiting factor in residential solar design, particularly in urban homes.

It also notes that network connection and export limits can restrict system design.

  • Source: Australian Government — sizing your solar system

The storage problem

Imagine a home with:

  • Limited sunny roof space
  • High evening electricity use
  • A desire for backup power
  • A household that may add an electric vehicle
  • More storage demand than the roof can regularly supply

The homeowner may want a larger battery.

But a larger stationary battery does not automatically mean more useful solar energy.

If there is not enough surplus solar to charge it, the battery may need to charge from the grid.

That may still be useful when:

  • Grid prices are low
  • Wholesale prices are favourable
  • The electricity plan rewards time-shifting
  • Backup capacity is the priority
  • The household needs energy overnight

But the financial model changes.

The system may no longer be based mainly on storing free rooftop solar.

It may rely more heavily on:

  • Grid arbitrage
  • Wholesale pricing
  • Retailer automation
  • Export revenue
  • Backup value
  • Battery cycling
  • Plan compatibility

Three ways to respond to limited roof space

When roof space limits solar production, there are several possible responses.

OPTION 1 — INSTALL A SMALLER BATTERY

A smaller battery may better match the amount of solar your roof can produce and the amount of energy your household uses later.

This may provide:

  • Lower upfront cost
  • Less unused capacity
  • Simpler operation
  • Lower battery cycling
  • A shorter or more credible payback

The downside is that the system may not provide the level of backup or evening coverage you wanted.

OPTION 2 — CHARGE A LARGER BATTERY FROM THE GRID

A larger battery can sometimes be charged from the grid when electricity prices are low.

This may be attractive on a wholesale-linked or time-of-use plan.

However, the result depends on:

  • Price differences
  • Plan fees
  • Battery efficiency
  • Battery throughput
  • Available charging windows
  • Export opportunities
  • Warranty conditions
  • Your ability to manage or automate the system

A larger battery charged from the grid is not the same financial proposition as a battery charged mainly from surplus solar.

OPTION 3 — USE THE EV AS ADDITIONAL STORAGE

A compatible electric vehicle may provide a much larger energy store than a typical home battery.

With the correct bidirectional charger and system configuration, the EV may be able to:

  • Charge from the grid
  • Charge from solar
  • Supply the home
  • Support backup power
  • Export to the grid
  • Participate in a retailer or VPP program

This is known as vehicle-to-everything, or V2X.

What is bidirectional charging?

A normal EV charger sends electricity into the vehicle.

A bidirectional charger allows energy to flow in both directions.

V2H — VEHICLE TO HOME

V2H allows the EV battery to supply electricity to the home.

It may be used to:

  • Reduce grid purchases
  • Supply the home during expensive periods
  • Support backup power during an outage
  • Use stored solar energy after dark

V2G — VEHICLE TO GRID

V2G allows the EV to send electricity to the grid.

It may be used to:

  • Export during high-value periods
  • Participate in retailer or VPP programs
  • Support the electricity network
  • Respond to wholesale prices

V2L — VEHICLE TO LOAD

V2L allows the vehicle to power individual appliances or equipment.

Examples include:

  • Tools
  • Camping equipment
  • Emergency appliances
  • Appliances connected through vehicle outlets

V2L is not the same as powering the entire home.

The Australian Government warns that not all EVs or charging systems support V2X, and that very few EVs currently have V2X capability confirmed by the vehicle manufacturer.

  • Source: Australian Government — vehicle-to-everything opportunities

The EV is not automatically a home battery

For bidirectional charging to work, several components must be compatible.

You need to check:

  • The EV model
  • The EV battery and software
  • The charging connector
  • The bidirectional charger
  • The home battery
  • The solar inverter
  • The energy management system
  • The backup equipment
  • The electricity plan
  • The distribution network
  • The vehicle warranty

A vehicle may support V2L but not V2H or V2G.

A vehicle may technically support bidirectional charging overseas but not have the feature enabled or supported in Australia.

A charger may support a vehicle but not integrate with your home battery.

A retailer may support V2G but not your vehicle or charger.

The full system must be checked.

The battery brand can affect your future options

Battery selection can determine the architecture of the rest of the home energy system.

Some manufacturers are building ecosystems that combine:

  • Solar inverter
  • Stationary battery
  • Battery management
  • Energy management system
  • Backup hardware
  • EV charger
  • Bidirectional charging
  • Retailer or VPP integration

Sigenergy, for example, promotes a SigenStor ecosystem that combines stationary storage and bidirectional EV charging options.

Other battery systems may rely on separate manufacturers and separate compatibility arrangements.

Neither approach is automatically better.

But the ecosystem should be considered before the first battery is purchased.

Ask:

  • Can the system support a bidirectional EV charger later?
  • Which EVs are supported?
  • Which charger is required?
  • Is the charger approved for connection in Queensland?
  • Can the EV supply the home during a grid outage?
  • Can the EV export to the grid?
  • Does the manufacturer support the configuration?
  • Does the battery warranty allow the planned operating mode?
  • Does the retailer or VPP support the complete system?

Queensland network approval matters

For Brisbane homes connected to the Energex network, bidirectional charging is not simply an appliance installation.

Energex states that a connection application must be submitted for bidirectional charging equipment connected to the electricity grid.

This is important because V2H and V2G equipment may export electricity back to the home electrical installation or the grid.

The system must be designed to:

  • Operate safely
  • Prevent dangerous backfeeding
  • Disconnect correctly during outages
  • Meet applicable inverter and connection requirements
  • Work with the property's phase and supply
  • Comply with Energex requirements
  • Source: Energex — bidirectional EV charging

Do not assume that a charger advertised as bidirectional is automatically approved for your property.

Ask the installer to confirm:

  • Whether an Energex application is required
  • Who will submit it
  • What equipment is being proposed
  • What export arrangement applies
  • Whether the installation can support V2H, V2G or both
  • Whether additional switchboard equipment is required

The vehicle warranty matters

Using an EV as a home or grid battery may increase the number of charge and discharge cycles.

That can affect:

  • Battery degradation
  • Vehicle warranty
  • Servicing
  • Manufacturer support
  • Minimum reserve settings
  • Driving range availability

The Australian Government advises homeowners to follow the vehicle manufacturer's information about V2X capabilities, installation, charger compatibility and charging practices.

Before using V2H or V2G, obtain written confirmation from the vehicle manufacturer or authorised representative that the intended use is supported.

Do not rely solely on the charger manufacturer saying that the vehicle is technically compatible.

You need to know whether the vehicle manufacturer supports the use under the vehicle warranty.

Why bidirectional charging can solve a roof-space problem

Bidirectional charging can provide additional energy storage without requiring additional rooftop panels.

That may be useful when:

  • The roof is already full
  • The household needs more storage
  • The EV is parked at home for long periods
  • The EV has a compatible battery and charging system
  • The household wants backup power
  • The electricity plan rewards charging and exporting at particular times
  • The solar system cannot be expanded economically

The EV effectively becomes an additional energy asset.

But it is only available when:

  • The vehicle is at home
  • The vehicle has enough charge
  • The owner permits discharge
  • The charger is operating
  • The network permits the connection
  • The retailer supports the arrangement
  • The vehicle warranty allows it

The EV is also a transport asset.

If you need the car for an early trip, preserving its driving range may matter more than exporting energy.

Expansion and bidirectional charging are connected

A homeowner may plan to:

  • Install solar now
  • Add a stationary battery
  • Add an EV later
  • Use the EV for V2H or V2G
  • Expand the energy system over time

That plan needs to be considered before the initial battery and inverter are selected.

Check:

  • Whether the battery can be expanded
  • Whether the inverter can support the extra capacity
  • Whether the energy management system can control both batteries
  • Whether the EV charger can integrate with the battery
  • Whether the charger is compatible with the vehicle
  • Whether the system can prioritise home backup
  • Whether the network permits the combined export
  • Whether the electricity plan supports the complete system

A battery system that is excellent as a standalone product may not be the best foundation for future bidirectional charging.

What to ask before buying the first battery

Ask your installer:

  • How much sunny roof space is available?
  • What is the maximum realistic solar system size?
  • How much energy will the roof produce across the year?
  • What battery size is actually supported by that production?
  • What happens if the household needs more storage later?
  • Can the battery be expanded?
  • How long do I have to expand it?
  • Can an EV later operate as a second battery?
  • Which bidirectional chargers are compatible?
  • Which EV models are supported?
  • Does the vehicle manufacturer support V2H or V2G?
  • Is the system approved for Energex connection?
  • Can it provide whole-home backup?
  • Can it export to the grid?
  • Which electricity plans can use the system?
  • Does the warranty permit bidirectional operation?
  • What happens if the EV is away from home?
  • What happens if the charger or software is offline?

Do not design a future system around a promise

“V2H-ready” can mean different things.

It may mean:

  • The system has a compatible inverter
  • A future charger may be added
  • The manufacturer intends to support the technology
  • The system has a communications pathway
  • A charger is available but not yet approved
  • The vehicle may be compatible
  • The system is waiting on software or network approval

Ask what is available today.

Ask what is only planned.

Ask what is supported by written warranty documentation.

Ask what has already been approved for your network.

The future opportunity may be significant, but the future system must not be treated as guaranteed.

The bottom line

Roof space can limit how much solar a home can generate.

A larger stationary battery may still be possible, but if it cannot be charged by surplus solar, the financial model may depend more heavily on grid charging, wholesale pricing or the electricity plan.

Bidirectional EV charging can provide another way to add storage without adding more panels.

But it depends on:

  • The vehicle
  • The charger
  • The battery ecosystem
  • The inverter
  • The network
  • The retailer
  • The warranty
  • The homeowner's driving needs

Bidirectional charging is not simply an EV feature.

It is a complete home energy system decision.

If future storage matters, choose the first battery with the future system in mind.
